Low-slope roofs carry most of Houston's businesses

Commercial roofing in Houston is mostly low-slope roofing: wide, nearly flat surfaces covered by a continuous membrane instead of overlapping shingles. The Whole Building Design Guide defines a low-slope roof as one with a pitch of 3 in 12 or less, and almost every strip center, office park and warehouse along the Beltway fits that description.

Those roofs fail differently from a house roof. Water does not run off quickly, so it finds open seams, clogged drains and cracked flashing around the equipment that sits on top. A small opening can wet a large area of insulation before anyone inside sees a stain on a ceiling tile.

What goes wrong on business roofs in this climate

Water that will not leave

NRCA treats a roof as properly sloped when no water is left standing 48 hours after rain in drying weather. Standing water beyond that is ponding. It adds weight, keeps seams wet and points to a low spot, a sagging deck or a blocked drain.

Drains, scuppers and gutters

Leaves, packaging, bird nests and loose gravel collect at roof drains. A blocked primary drain sends water toward the overflow scuppers or over the parapet, and the water left behind loads the roof.

Rooftop units and curbs

Air conditioning units, exhaust fans and vents sit on curbs that are flashed into the membrane. Foot traffic to and from those units, dropped screws and tools, and panels left off can all damage the membrane around them.

Seams and edges in the wind

Wind lifts a membrane first at its edges and corners. Loose metal edging or coping lets air get under the sheet, and a seam that was weak from the start opens further each storm.

Wet insulation

Water that gets under the membrane soaks the board insulation and stays there. Wet insulation loses its value, adds weight and can rot a wood deck or rust a steel one. It also limits whether a coating or recover is possible.

Heat and sun

Dark roofs run far hotter than light ones. ENERGY STAR cites Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory: on a summer afternoon a clean white roof reflecting 80 percent of sunlight stays about 50 degrees Fahrenheit cooler than a gray roof reflecting 20 percent.

How a careful roofer handles a commercial job

  1. A roof walk and an interior check. Seams, flashing, curbs, drains, edges and the underside of the deck where it is visible, with the leak history from tenants or maintenance staff.
  2. Finding out how wet the roof is. On a roof of any size, the roofer should tell you how it measured moisture under the membrane and show the results on a roof plan.
  3. Options in writing. Repair, coating, recover over the existing roof or a full tear-off, each with its scope, its warranty and what it assumes about the deck and insulation.
  4. A plan for the occupants. Working hours, crane or lift locations, odors from adhesives or torch work, noise, parking, and who tells the tenants.
  5. Permit, inspections and closeout. The permit pulled in the roofer's name, inspections passed, and a closeout file with photos, the manufacturer's warranty if one applies and the workmanship warranty.

Commercial roofing permits in Houston

Inside the City of Houston, a new roof or an overlay on a commercial structure needs a commercial roofing permit (Houston Permitting Center code HPWCODE1157). The city lists plan review and inspection as required, a processing time of about 10 business days and a permit that stays valid for six months. The application includes a building permit application and either the Commercial Re-Roof Only Worksheet (CE-1076) or the roofing overlay worksheet (CE-1104), plus plans where they apply. Depending on the scope, the structural, planning, fire marshal or floodplain groups may also need to approve it, and the permit, worksheet and plans must stay on site for the inspector.

Insulation matters to the permit as well. The city's commercial re-roofing guidance asks for an energy code compliance report with a completed inspection checklist when the insulation on the deck changes. Ask the roofer, before you sign, whether the job will touch the insulation and how that affects the permit and the price.

Suburban cities such as Sugar Land, Pearland, League City and Baytown run their own building departments with their own rules, so the roofer should confirm the local requirement in writing. The service areas page links to each town page.

Buildings on the coast

Commercial buildings near the bay follow the same windstorm rules as homes. The Texas Department of Insurance says a commercial structure that will be insured for wind and hail through the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association must at minimum meet TDI's adopted building standards, and TWIA counts repairs, including reroofing, among the improvements that need certification. TDI's product evaluations for metal roof panels require uplift testing to a recognized standard such as UL 580 or UL 1897.

Questions for owners and property managers to ask

About the roof and the proposal

  • How did you check for wet insulation, and where is it wet on the roof plan?
  • Why this system and this attachment method for this building?
  • What happens at each drain, curb, edge and wall in the scope?
  • Does the manufacturer's warranty require its own inspection, and who pays for it?
  • How will rooftop units be disconnected, lifted or protected, and who calls the mechanical contractor?

About the company and the site

  • Can you send a certificate of liability insurance and proof of workers' compensation coverage, naming my company as certificate holder?
  • Will you pull the permit, and who schedules the inspections?
  • What hours will you work, and how will tenants, parking and deliveries be handled?
  • Where will materials, the dumpster and any crane go?
  • What maintenance plan do you suggest after the job, and what does it cost per visit?

If a storm damaged the roof and the building is insured, read the roof insurance claims guide. The policyholder files the claim, and Texas law limits what a roofer may do in that process.

Keeping a commercial roof working between projects

Most low-slope roofs that fail early do so from neglect rather than a single storm. NRCA's recommendation of an inspection at least twice a year, preferably spring and fall, plus a look after any hurricane, tornado or hailstorm, fits Houston well: spring comes before the worst of the thunderstorm and hurricane season, and fall shows what that season did.

  • Keep drains, strainers and scuppers clear, especially under trees and after storms.
  • Limit roof access to people who need to be there, and log who goes up.
  • Ask mechanical contractors to report any damage they cause or see.
  • Keep the warranty, drawings and every inspection report in one file.
  • Fix small seam and flashing problems while they are small; wet insulation is what turns a repair into a replacement.

The May 16, 2024 derecho brought winds over 100 mph to parts of the Houston area, according to the National Weather Service office in League City, and broke windows in downtown towers. Hurricane Beryl followed on July 8, 2024. Many commercial roofs took debris and edge damage that year. If your building has not had a roof inspection since then, it is worth one.
