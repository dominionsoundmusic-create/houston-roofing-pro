Wind damage hides under shingles that look fine

Wind damage to a roof is often a broken bond you cannot see. Asphalt shingles are nailed down and then glued to the course below by a strip of sealant that softens in the sun. Once wind pries that bond loose, the shingle is held only by its nails.

The Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety (IBHS) says seal strength is the biggest factor in how self-sealing shingles perform in high wind. That is why a roof can lose very few shingles in a storm and still be badly weakened: dozens of tabs can be unsealed and lying flat, waiting for the next gust to fold them back or for sideways rain to run beneath them.

The three kinds of wind damage roofers find

Broken seals

The tab looks normal but lifts freely by hand, often with a clean strip of sealant that no longer grips anything. It is the most common and the easiest to miss. A careful roofer lifts tabs gently on each slope and photographs the ones that come up without resistance.

Creased tabs

A tab that was bent up and back by a gust keeps a horizontal fold line across it, and the mat may be cracked along the crease. A creased shingle will not lie tight again and needs replacing, along with a look at the nails that held it.

Missing shingles and caps

The visible kind. Bare underlayment or exposed nail heads where tabs tore off, ridge caps in the yard, a strip missing along an edge. Bare spots let rain straight into the underlayment, so these need covering before the next storm.

Where a roofer looks first

Wind does not load a roof evenly. Roofers usually start at the exposed edges and work inward, because that is where damage tends to begin and spread.

Part of the roofWhat the roofer checks
Eaves and rakesThe lower edge and the sloped side edges, where wind can catch the lip of the first course. The starter strip and drip edge should be fastened and sealed.
CornersWhere an eave meets a rake. Lifting here often spreads into the field of the roof.
Ridge and hipsThe caps along the peak and the outside corners of a hip roof. Caps that sit crooked or open at the ends have lost their grip.
The slopes that faced the stormGusts from one direction break seals on the slopes that took them.
Around earlier repairsA patch with new shingles that never sealed to their neighbors, or tabs lifted and dropped by a past storm, gives the next gust a handle.

Ask the roofer to mark each damaged spot on a simple roof sketch or in labeled photos. That is the record your adjuster will want, and it keeps a repair focused on what the wind actually did.

Checking for wind damage without going up

Stay off the roof. Unsealed shingles slide, and a wet roof gives no grip. Everything below can be done from the yard, the street and the attic doorway.

  • Sight along the ridge from the street. Caps should form a straight, even line. A raised end or a gap shows against the sky.
  • Look at the edges in low sun. Early morning or late afternoon light throws a shadow under a lifted tab that midday light hides.
  • Walk the yard and the beds. Pieces of shingle, cap or drip edge mean something came off. Photograph them where they landed.
  • Look into the attic from the hatch. Without stepping onto the joists, look for daylight along the edges or fresh water stains on the underside of the deck.
  • Note the date and time. Write down when the strong wind came through. Keep any weather alert you received on your phone.

Binoculars help. A phone camera zoomed from across the street is often clearer still, and the photo is dated.

Why wind damage is easy to underrate in Houston

People here tend to measure storms by whether they had a name. The roofs say otherwise. Forecasters at the Houston/Galveston weather service office described the derecho of May 16, 2024 as a swath of winds over 100 mph through the metro, and its survey teams found that much of the damage came from straight-line thunderstorm winds rather than a tornado or hurricane. Less than two months later Hurricane Beryl came ashore southwest of Houston on July 8, 2024 as a Category 1 storm with 80 mph sustained winds, according to the National Hurricane Center.

A seal that broke in either storm does not glue itself back. If your roof has not been looked at closely since 2024, the first sign may be a stain after a sideways rain, or a row of tabs that finally folds back in an ordinary afternoon squall.

Documenting wind damage for a claim

Wind is harder to prove than hail because a broken seal leaves no mark. Insurers distinguish storm damage from wear, so the file needs to tie fresh damage to a storm date. Ask the roofer for:

  1. Photos of tabs being lifted by hand, showing the clean sealant strip, slope by slope.
  2. Photos of creases and tears with a marker or tape for scale.
  3. A count by slope of the damaged shingles, and the direction each slope faces.
  4. The storm date it believes caused the damage, and what it is based on.

Then you file with your insurer and decide when work starts. The storm claim checklist is a printable list for the paperwork, and the roof insurance claims guide explains deductibles and payment.

Repair or replace after wind

Usually a repair

  • A few missing or creased shingles on one or two slopes
  • Lifted ridge caps on a roof that is otherwise sound
  • Unsealed tabs in one area that can be resealed or replaced
  • Matching shingles are available or close enough to accept

Worth pricing a new roof

  • Broken seals across several slopes
  • Old, brittle shingles that crack when a tab is lifted
  • Repeated lifting after earlier repairs
  • No matching shingle, and a patch that would stand out

If a roof is replaced after wind, ask the roofer which shingle wind rating it is quoting, how many nails go in each shingle, and how the edges and starter course will be fastened. Those details decide how the next storm goes.

Questions to ask about a wind repair

  • How many shingles on each slope lifted freely by hand, and can I see the photos?
  • Are any shingles creased or torn, and will you replace those rather than reseal them?
  • Is the decking sound along the edges where shingles came off?
  • Can you match my shingles, and what will you do if you cannot?
  • Will you send your certificate of liability insurance and pull any permit my city requires?
