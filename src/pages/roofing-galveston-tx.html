An island where every roof is a coastal roof

Roofing in Galveston, TX is not the same job as roofing in the suburbs north of the causeway. The whole island sits inside the Texas Department of Insurance's designated catastrophe area, salt spray rides in on the Gulf breeze, and some of the oldest houses in greater Houston stand inside local historic districts with their own review for roof materials.

That means a Galveston re-roof usually involves three things beyond shingles and nails: a city permit, a state windstorm inspection while the roof is going on, and, on many blocks of the East End and Silk Stocking, a Landmark Commission review before any old roofing comes off. A roofer that works the island regularly will know all three. One that drove down from the mainland for the week may not.

The WPI-8, step by step on a Galveston re-roof

Galveston is unusual because the city writes the state certificate into its own rules: the Building Division says a WPI-8 from TDI is required on completion of all new construction, solar panels, siding and roofing replacement. So the windstorm inspection is not only an insurance matter here. It is part of closing out the job with the city.

  1. Before the tear-off. TDI asks that it be told about the project before work begins, using its application form WPI-1. Ask the roofer to show you that it has been filed.
  2. While the roof is open. A TDI inspector or a TDI-appointed Texas licensed engineer checks the work during installation, when decking, underlayment and the nailing pattern can still be seen.
  3. When it passes. TDI issues the WPI-8 certificate of compliance, and the city expects it at completion.
  4. If a step was missed. A Texas licensed professional engineer can inspect after the fact, and TDI may issue a WPI-8-E. That costs time and money a planned inspection would have saved.
  5. Check it yourself. TDI's website has a lookup for WPI-8 and WPI-8-E certificates. Search your address once the job is done.

Historic districts: when the roof is part of the history

Galveston has four local historic districts. The East End, created in 1971, covers about 66 blocks. Silk Stocking, created in 1975, runs along 25th Street, which locals also call Rosenberg. Lost Bayou, designated in 1994, covers about 23 blocks and takes its name from Hitchcock's Bayou, which was filled in during the 1880s. Strand/Mechanic is the fourth, and the only one that is mostly commercial.

Inside those districts, the city reviews exterior work against its Design Standards for Historic Properties, which are based on the Secretary of the Interior's Standards for Rehabilitation. Landmark Commission staff reports from recent years show how that plays out for roofs:

  • Original roofing that can still be repaired should be repaired, not torn off.
  • A replacement should be historically appropriate, and where records of the original roof exist, similar in size, shape, texture and color.
  • Metal roofs can be approved when they are detailed to fit the buildings around them, and composition shingle is offered as an option.
  • Rolled roofing is limited to flat roofs.
  • A decision can be appealed, but only by letter within 10 days.

The commission has approved some material changes and recommended against others, depending on the house. Before you sign a contract for a historic house, ask the roofer whether it has taken a roof through the Certificate of Appropriateness process, and check the current standards with the city's historic preservation staff. A change of material can also change the windstorm details, so both reviews have to agree.

Salt, sun and fasteners

Wind gets the headlines, but salt does damage every day. FEMA's technical bulletin on corrosion in coastal areas says salt spray is heaviest near breaking waves and drops off quickly over the first 300 to 3,000 feet inland. For exposed roof connectors and fasteners inside that 3,000-foot band, FEMA recommends hot-dip galvanized or stainless steel, and it warns that mechanically galvanized coatings are thinner and more brittle. On a narrow island, a large share of homes sit inside that band.

Corroded nails, clips and drip edge do not always show from the ground. A roof can look tidy while the metal holding it down has thinned. That is one reason a Galveston inspection should include close photos of fasteners and flashing, and why a quote should name the metal for each part.

Raised houses and beach homes

The beach houses of the West End and the Bolivar side stand on tall pilings, which puts the roof higher off the ground and more exposed to wind than a slab house on the mainland. Getting materials and a crew up safely takes planning. Ask how the roofer will protect decks, railings and stairs while the work is under way.

Questions to ask a roofer about a Galveston roof

Paperwork

  • Are you registered with the City of Galveston, and will you pull the permit in your name?
  • Who files with TDI before work starts, and who schedules the windstorm inspections?
  • When will I have the WPI-8, and will you send me a copy?
  • Is my house in a local historic district, and have you taken a roof through the Landmark Commission before?

Materials

  • What are the nails, clips, drip edge and flashing made of, given how close I am to the water?
  • Which shingles or panels will you use, and are they rated for the wind speeds in the coastal code?
  • What will you do if you find soft decking, and what is the price per sheet?
  • Can you send me your certificate of liability insurance and a written workmanship warranty?
