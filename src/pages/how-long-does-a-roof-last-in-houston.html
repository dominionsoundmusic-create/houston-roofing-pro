The honest answer: it depends on four things

How long does a roof last in Houston? Usually less than the number printed on the shingle bundle, and how much less depends on the material, the installation, the attic underneath it and the storms it lives through.

A roof here works harder than the same roof in a mild, dry climate. It bakes under a strong sun for much of the year, stays damp in humid air, and takes wind and hail from spring thunderstorms and hurricane season. Two identical roofs put on the same week can end their lives years apart: one shaded and well ventilated with no storm damage, the other in full sun over a hot attic, hit by hail in its fifth year.

This guide explains what each part of Houston's weather does to a roof, how the common materials compare, what you can do from the ground to help, and the signs that a roof is near the end.

What Houston weather does to a roof

Sun and heat

Sunlight breaks down the asphalt in shingles over time, and the mineral granules on top are what shield it. As granules wear away, the asphalt underneath ages faster, dries out and cracks. Heat speeds that up. Rubber pipe collars and sealants age the same way, which is why a cracked vent boot is one of the most common leaks on an otherwise sound Houston roof.

Humidity and rain

Climate normals for 1991 to 2020 from the National Weather Service for Bush Intercontinental Airport put average yearly rain at about 52 inches. Wet wood stays wet longer in humid air, so a small leak can soften decking and rafters faster than you would expect. Valleys under trees, where leaves hold water against the roof, are a frequent starting point.

Algae

The dark streaks on many Houston roofs are not dirt. The Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association says they are mainly algae, most often Gloeocapsa magma, which thrives in warm, humid places. Whatever the streaks look like, cleaning them the wrong way can cost the roof years: ARMA's 2024 bulletin tells owners never to aim a high-pressure washer at a shingle roof, because it is likely to cause damage.

Wind

Shingles rely on an adhesive strip to stay sealed to the course below. The Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety says seal strength is the biggest factor in how shingles hold up in high wind, and seals weaken with age. A roof that rode out storms in its early years may lose shingles in a smaller one later. NOAA dates the Atlantic hurricane season from the first of June through the end of November.

Hail

Hail can crack shingles and knock off granules in one afternoon, taking years off the roof even when nothing leaks right away. Bruised spots then age faster than the shingles around them. After a hailstorm, look for dents in gutters, vents and the air conditioning unit, and have the roof checked if you find them. See hail damage roof repair.

Big storms

Many Houston roofs end their lives early in a single event. On May 16, 2024, the National Weather Service recorded a derecho with winds above 100 mph across the metro, and Hurricane Beryl made landfall near Matagorda as a Category 1 storm on July 8, 2024. For a lot of homeowners, that summer, not age, decided when the roof was replaced.

The gap between the two charts is the point: the same shingle is expected to last far less on a hot, humid coast than the standard chart suggests. Some inspectors think the coastal chart's figure for metal is too low, so treat every number here as a starting point for a conversation with a roofer, not a date. Older summaries of a National Association of Home Builders study put asphalt shingle roofs at about 20 years and note that hail and hurricanes can cut any roof's life short.

A shingle warranty is not a forecast of how long the roof will last. Many warranties limit what they cover as the years pass, and storm damage from wind beyond the rating or from hail is usually an insurance matter, not a warranty one. Read the warranty document itself before you choose a product. The roof replacement page covers the rest of a new roof.

The attic is half the story

The roof covering sits on top of an attic, and in a Houston summer that attic can get very hot. Air that cannot move traps heat under the deck and bakes the shingles from below as well as from above, and moist air that cannot escape condenses on cool surfaces in winter. Both shorten a roof's life.

A working attic pulls cooler air in low, usually through vents in the soffits under the eaves, and lets hot air out high, through ridge vents, turbines or box vents. Insulation blown over the soffit vents, a bathroom fan that dumps humid air into the attic instead of outside, or a mix of exhaust vent types that work against each other can all upset that flow.

You can learn a lot from the attic hatch without walking into the attic: look for water stains on the underside of the deck, daylight where there should be none, dark or damp wood, and insulation pressed tight against the eaves. When a roofer prices a new roof, ask how it will vent the attic and whether the intake and exhaust are balanced.

Signs a Houston roof is near the end

All of these can be seen from the ground, from a window or from inside the house. Do not climb up to look. If you see several of them at once, it is time for a roofer to look closely.

From the yard

  • Shingle edges curling up or corners cupping across much of the roof
  • Bare, darker patches where granules have worn away
  • Cracked or missing shingles after storms that did not used to bother the roof
  • A wavy roof line or a dip between rafters, which can mean soft decking
  • Rusted or lifted flashing around chimneys and walls

Around and inside the house

  • Granules collecting in gutters and at the bottom of downspouts
  • Ceiling stains in more than one room, or stains that return after a repair
  • Daylight or water stains visible from the attic hatch
  • Repairs needed every season instead of every few years
  • Records showing the roof is original on an older home

What helps a roof last, done from the ground

Look after every big storm

Walk around the house after strong wind or hail. Look for shingles in the yard, dented gutters and vents, and new stains inside. Photograph what you find. A small storm repair done promptly costs less than the rot that follows a slow leak.

Keep water moving

Clogged gutters push water back under the roof's edge. Have them cleared by someone with the right equipment, especially in fall when oaks and pines drop leaves and needles, and have limbs over the roof trimmed by a tree service.

Clean gently or not at all

If algae streaks bother you, know that ARMA warns against pressure washing and scrubbing shingles, and GAF's technical bulletin on algae also warns against power washing. ARMA treats zinc or copper strips as something to add when a roof is replaced, not to nail onto an existing roof, and algae-resistant shingles are another option at replacement.

A roof inspection every few years after the roof's early life, and after any hailstorm, lets a roofer find failing pipe boots, loose flashing and lifted shingles while they are still small. The roof inspection page explains what a thorough one includes.

Roof age, insurance and replacing on your own schedule

A roof's age matters to more than the roof. When you shop for or renew home insurance, the insurer will usually ask how old the roof is. Ask your agent or insurer these questions before a storm forces the issue:

  • Does my policy pay a roof claim at replacement cost or at actual cash value, which subtracts depreciation for age?
  • Is there an endorsement or roof schedule that changes how the roof is paid as it ages?
  • What is my wind and hail deductible, and is it a percentage of the home's insured value?
  • Would impact-resistant shingles change my premium?

Replacing a worn roof on your own schedule, before it fails, lets you get several written estimates, choose the product and pick the season, instead of competing with every neighbor for a roofer after a storm. If you live in a deed-restricted neighborhood, check your HOA's rules on colors and materials before you sign. Texas Property Code Section 202.011 limits an HOA's power to prohibit certain impact-resistant and energy-efficient shingles that look like the shingles it allows. In the coastal catastrophe area, a new roof also needs a windstorm inspection.
