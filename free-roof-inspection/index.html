What "free" actually buys, and what it does not

Free roof inspections are genuinely free, and there is no catch in the sense people worry about. The inspection is a sales call, and that is fine — the contractor is spending an hour hoping you need work. Most of the time they find you do not, and they leave.

What it is not is an independent assessment. The person telling you whether you need a roof is the person who would sell you one. That is not dishonest, it is just worth naming, and it changes what you should ask for.

Ask for photographs of the specific damage found, not a verdict. A contractor who has genuinely inspected your roof can show you the bruised shingle, the cracked boot, the lifted flashing. One who cannot produce photographs did not look closely.

What a real inspection covers

  • The roof surface — shingle condition, granule loss, curling, lifted or missing pieces, and test squares if hail is suspected.
  • Every penetration — pipe boots, vents, chimney and skylight flashing, valleys. Most leaks start at one of these, not in the field of the roof.
  • The gutters and soft metal, which record hail hits more clearly than shingles do.
  • The attic. This is the half that gets skipped, and it is where the truth is. Water staining on the decking, daylight, compressed or wet insulation, and whether the ventilation is actually working.
  • A written report with photographs, whether or not you buy anything.

An inspection that never enters the attic is half an inspection. Ask specifically.

When to get one

  • After any hailstorm in your area, even if the roof looks untouched from the ground. Hail bruising is usually invisible from the driveway.
  • After high winds, particularly if neighbours lost shingles.
  • Before buying a house. A general home inspection looks at a roof briefly and from a distance. A roofing inspection is a different thing.
  • Before selling, so a buyer's inspector does not produce the first surprise.
  • Every couple of years past year ten, simply to know where you stand.

Inspection versus what an adjuster does

They look for different things. A contractor is assessing whether the roof needs work. An insurance adjuster is assessing whether a covered peril caused damage on a specific date, which is a narrower question.

A contractor's report supports a claim but does not substitute for the adjuster's visit. The useful move is having your contractor present when the adjuster comes — two people on the same roof find more than one, and a contractor who knows the test-square method can point out damage on slopes the adjuster has not walked.

Questions worth asking

  • Did you go in the attic?
  • Can I see photographs of what you found?
  • Is this repairable, and what would a repair cost against a replacement?
  • If you are recommending replacement, which of the signs are you basing that on?
  • Are you licensed and insured, and how long have you been at this address?
