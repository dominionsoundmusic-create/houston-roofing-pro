Roof Repair in Missouri City, TX

Missouri City is fifty years of building in one city, and the roof on a Quail Valley ranch has almost nothing in common with the roof on a Sienna new build. Knowing which one you have changes the whole conversation.

Quail Valley and the older neighbourhoods

Built largely in the 1970s, and by now on the third or fourth roof. At that point the shingles are the least interesting part of the assessment.

  • Layer count. Most codes allow two layers of shingles. A house already at two must be stripped to the decking, and tear-off of two layers costs meaningfully more than one.
  • Decking type. Some homes of this vintage have plank decking with gaps between boards rather than continuous plywood. Fasteners have less to grip.
  • Accumulated repairs. Fifty years of roofing cement over flashing, vents sealed rather than replaced, patches from storms nobody remembers.
  • Ventilation. Added piecemeal if at all, frequently exhaust with no adequate soffit intake, which does very little.

Verify the layer count before you accept a quote on an older Missouri City home. A contractor who assumes one layer and finds three has an expensive conversation with you on the morning of the job, and the price he quoted is not the price you pay.

Sienna and the newer sections

A completely different situation. These roofs are on their first replacement cycle, the decking is uniform plywood or OSB, and tear-off is straightforward.

What they have instead is full sun exposure. New master-planned sections have young landscaping and nothing tall enough to shade a roof for another twenty years, so the shingles take the entire ultraviolet load every day. Builder-grade three-tab rated for twenty-five years commonly gives fifteen to eighteen here.

The other newer-build issue is ventilation that meets code and no more. A ridge vent with undersized soffit intake cannot move air, and an attic that stays superheated cooks the shingles from beneath.

The clay underneath

Fort Bend County clay swells and shrinks with the seasons, and over decades that movement works on a house. It rarely damages a roof directly, but it can open small gaps where the roof meets a wall or a chimney — which is exactly where flashing has to stay sealed.

If you have visible foundation movement and a leak near a chimney or a wall junction, the two are probably related.

Before you call

  • Which neighbourhood, and roughly when the house was built.
  • Whether the roof is original or a replacement, and how many times.
  • Whether the leak is near a wall, chimney or in the open field.
  • Whether the attic has ever been assessed for ventilation.
